How Much Does a Whole-Home Renovation Cost in Brantford and Brant County?

If your kitchen, bathrooms and basement all feel out of date at the same time, you're probably not looking at three separate projects — you're looking at one whole-home renovation. It's usually the more efficient way to tackle a house that no longer works room by room, but it's also the largest number a homeowner attaches to a property that isn't new construction.

We hear a version of this question from almost every homeowner planning something bigger than a single room: "What would it cost to just do the whole house?" Usually they've already priced out a kitchen, a bathroom or two, and a basement separately, added the numbers, and been surprised — either because the total feels impossible, or because it's closer to a full rebuild than they expected.

There's no single number that answers this honestly without seeing the property. But we can walk through the ranges we actually see on whole-home projects in Brantford and Brant County, what pushes a project toward the low end or the high end, and how to think about tackling it in one project versus several.

What counts as a whole-home renovation

"Whole-home renovation" gets used loosely, so it's worth being specific. For pricing purposes, we treat it as one coordinated project that touches most or all of a house at once — typically the kitchen, every bathroom, flooring and trim throughout the main living areas, and often a finished or upgraded basement. That's different from a main-floor renovation, which focuses on the kitchen and connected living space but leaves the upstairs bedrooms alone. It's also different from a home addition, which adds new square footage rather than upgrading what already exists — additions and ARUs are priced separately and covered on our additions and ARUs page.

Most whole-home projects also end up touching systems the homeowner never sees day to day: the electrical panel, plumbing stacks, HVAC distribution, and insulation. In a home built after roughly 2000, those systems are usually adequate and get left alone. In an older Brantford or Paris home, updating at least some of them is often part of the scope whether the homeowner planned for it going in or not.

For a typical 1,800–2,400 sq ft home, here's the range of what we see across three tiers of scope. These are all-in numbers — labour, materials, permits where required, and a reasonable contingency — for a home in average-to-good structural condition. A property with significant hidden issues will run higher.

As a rough rule of thumb, whole-home renovations in Brantford and Brant County run somewhere in the range of $150–$300 per square foot of finished area, depending on how much structural work, system upgrades and finish-level selections are involved. A cosmetic-only refresh comes in under that range; a full structural gut of an older home with premium finishes can exceed it. None of this replaces an actual walkthrough — the only number worth planning around is one based on your specific house.

What actually drives the cost

Two homes of the same square footage can land $150,000 apart in final cost. The gap usually comes down to a handful of factors:

  • Structural changes. Removing a load-bearing wall between the kitchen and living room means an engineered beam, a structural review, and shoring during construction — not just demo and drywall.
  • Plumbing and electrical relocation. Moving a kitchen sink to an island, adding a bathroom where none existed, or upgrading an old 100-amp panel to 200 amps all add real cost before a single finish gets chosen.
  • Existing conditions. Homes built before the 1980s more often need wiring, plumbing, and insulation addressed as part of the project instead of as an optional add-on.
  • Material and finish selections. Stock cabinets and porcelain tile versus custom cabinetry and natural stone can shift a single room's cost by tens of thousands of dollars. Across a whole home, that gap compounds fast.
  • Occupancy during construction. Staying in the home while it's renovated slows the schedule and adds cost for phased protection and sequencing work around daily life. Moving out, where it's possible, is usually faster and sometimes cheaper overall.
  • Permits. A whole-home renovation involving structural work typically requires a building permit, and may need separate plumbing and electrical permits reviewed on their own timelines.

Staged renovation or one continuous project?

Most homeowners can't or don't want to write one cheque for a whole-home renovation. The real choice is usually staged versus continuous, and both are legitimate strategies.

Staging the project — one or two rooms at a time over two to four years — spreads out the cash flow and lets you keep living in most of the house at any given point. The trade-off is that it usually costs more cumulatively. Each phase means remobilizing trades, and decisions made years apart are harder to keep cohesive: flooring that matched perfectly when you installed it in the kitchen may be discontinued by the time you get to the basement three years later.

Doing it as one continuous project — typically four to eight months depending on scope — usually costs less overall because trades mobilize once, materials get ordered together, and design decisions get made as a set instead of piecemeal. We saw this play out directly on a recent main-level renovation in Brantford, where the homeowners chose to run the kitchen, laundry room, flooring and bathroom work as one coordinated project rather than phasing it — the trades came through once instead of twice, and later work never had to route around finishes that were already in place.

If the budget is available and you can tolerate several months of real disruption, one continuous project is almost always the more cost-efficient and more cohesive path. If it isn't, staging is the honest answer — just plan the sequence deliberately so later phases don't force you to redo earlier finishes.

If staging is the plan, tell us the full scope up front even if you're only budgeting for phase one. We can price and sequence the whole project so early decisions don't box in later ones.

What changes in older Brantford and Paris homes

Brantford and Paris both have a meaningful stock of homes built well before 1980, and older homes change the math on a whole-home renovation. Knob-and-tube or early aluminum wiring, galvanized or cast iron plumbing, uneven or settled framing, and insulation that doesn't meet current standards are all common enough that we plan for the possibility rather than treat it as a surprise. Previous unpermitted work — a finished basement from decades ago, a moved wall with no engineering behind it — also turns up more often than homeowners expect.

We generally recommend budgeting a 10–15% contingency on a whole-home renovation in a pre-1980 Brantford or Paris home, versus 5–10% for a home built after that. It isn't a guarantee something will go wrong — it's what lets a genuine surprise get absorbed without stalling the project or blowing the budget.

Rural Brant County considerations

Renovations in rural Brant County carry a few additional variables that don't apply inside Brantford's urban service area. If the renovation adds bathrooms, bedrooms, or overall fixture count, a septic system sized for the home's original layout may need to be reviewed before the project is approved. Well capacity matters for the same reason if you're adding fixtures. Material delivery and site access can also affect scheduling on rural properties with long driveways or limited turnaround space for trucks.

Permitting in Brant County goes through the County of Brant's building department, which is a separate municipality from the City of Brantford. The permit process is broadly similar, but timelines and specific requirements can differ, so it's worth confirming with the county directly for anything beyond a cosmetic-only scope.

What to do before you request an estimate

  • Walk the house room by room and separate "must fix now" from "nice to have later."
  • Decide whether staged or continuous fits your actual cash flow — not the timeline you'd prefer in an ideal world.
  • Set aside a written contingency line, especially if the home was built before 1980.
  • In rural Brant County, have septic and well capacity checked before finalizing scope.
  • Book a site visit instead of asking for a number over the phone — nothing here replaces seeing the actual house.
