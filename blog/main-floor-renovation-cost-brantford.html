Main-Floor Renovation Cost in Brantford: Kitchen, Flooring and Structural Changes

If your kitchen renovation keeps growing to include the flooring in the dining room, the wall between the kitchen and living room, and the powder room off the hallway, you're not pricing a kitchen anymore — you're pricing a main-floor renovation. Here's what that actually costs in Brantford, and what pushes the number up or down.

We hear this one constantly: a homeowner calls about a kitchen renovation, and by the second conversation the scope has quietly expanded to the flooring in the dining room, the wall separating the kitchen from the living room, and sometimes the powder room down the hall. That's not scope creep — it's a different project. A main-floor renovation touches the kitchen and the living spaces connected to it as one coordinated job, and it's priced differently than a kitchen renovation contained to its existing footprint.

For a typical Brantford home, a main-floor renovation that includes a full kitchen, new flooring carried through the connected living and dining space, and removing a wall to open the layout runs $150,000–$260,000. A cosmetic-only main-floor refresh with no layout changes starts closer to $60,000. A major structural remodel with multiple wall removals and higher-end finishes can exceed $400,000. Below is where those numbers come from and what moves a project between tiers.

What counts as a main-floor renovation

We use "main-floor renovation" for a project that touches the kitchen plus the connected living and dining space as one job — new flooring carried through all of it, coordinated finishes, and often a wall removed to open the layout. It's a bigger scope than a kitchen renovation contained to the kitchen's existing footprint, but a smaller scope than a whole-home renovation, which also touches every bathroom and typically the basement.

The distinction matters because pricing a main-floor project as if it were "just a kitchen" is one of the most common ways a budget gets blown early. Flooring that has to match and transition cleanly across three or four rooms, electrical that has to be extended beyond the kitchen footprint, and a structural opening that affects ceiling and lighting layout in the adjoining room all add cost that a kitchen-only quote won't capture.

Main-floor renovation cost in Brantford: the ranges

These ranges assume a typical Brantford or Paris main level of roughly 500–800 sq ft of combined kitchen, living and dining space, in a home with no major hidden structural or system issues. They're all-in figures — labour, materials, permits where required, and a reasonable contingency.

Scope Typical Cost What's included
Main-floor refresh $60,000 – $100,000 New flooring and trim throughout the main level, updated kitchen cabinet fronts, counters and backsplash, paint and lighting. No layout changes.
Full main-floor renovation $150,000 – $260,000 Full kitchen renovation, one wall removed with an engineered beam, new flooring and trim carried through the connected living and dining space, updated electrical.

As a rough rule of thumb, main-floor renovations in Brantford run somewhere around $130–$220 per square foot of the main-level area being renovated, depending on how much structural work and finish-level selection is involved. That's a narrower footprint than a whole-home project, which is why the total is lower even though the per-square-foot rate for the structural tiers is similar. None of these figures replace an actual walkthrough — every property is different.

What actually drives the cost

Two main-floor projects of similar square footage can land tens of thousands of dollars apart. The gap usually comes down to a handful of factors:

  • Structural changes. Removing the wall between the kitchen and living room means an engineered beam, a structural review, and often shoring during construction — not just demo and drywall.
  • Flooring continuity. Carrying one floor across the kitchen, dining room, living room and hallway means levelling transitions between rooms that may currently sit at different subfloor heights, and sourcing enough material in one dye lot to avoid visible seams.
  • Plumbing and electrical relocation. Moving a kitchen sink to an island, extending lighting circuits into the newly opened living space, or adding a powder room all add cost before a single finish gets chosen.
  • Existing conditions. Homes built before the 1980s more often need wiring, subfloor or insulation addressed once walls and floors are opened up, whether or not it was part of the original plan.
  • Material and finish selections. Stock cabinets and laminate flooring versus custom millwork and engineered hardwood can shift a main-floor project's cost by $50,000 or more.
  • Occupancy during construction. Living in the home while the main floor is under construction — with no functioning kitchen and reduced living space — slows the schedule and adds cost for phased protection and sequencing.
  • Permits. Any wall removal, electrical panel work or plumbing relocation typically requires a permit from the City of Brantford or the County of Brant, reviewed on its own timeline.

When a kitchen renovation becomes a main-floor renovation

There's a specific point where a kitchen project's scope crosses into main-floor territory, and it's worth recognizing early because it changes both the price and the sequencing. Watch for these signs:

  • You want the kitchen to feel "open" to the living room — which almost always means the wall between them has to come down.
  • The existing kitchen flooring doesn't match what's in the adjoining room, and a new kitchen floor would create an awkward transition or dead-end at the doorway.
  • The powder room, laundry room or a hallway closet sits along the same wall run as the kitchen and would need to shift as part of an open layout.
  • You're planning an island, and the electrical and plumbing runs to support it extend past where the current kitchen footprint ends.

None of this means every kitchen project should become a main-floor project — plenty of homeowners get a great result staying within the existing footprint, at kitchen-only pricing. But if two or more of these apply, it's worth pricing the main-floor scope from the start rather than discovering it mid-project.

Flooring: the line item that grows with the project

Flooring is the part of a main-floor renovation that homeowners most often underbudget, because it looks like a simple per-square-foot cost until it has to run through several rooms at different heights, around a new structural opening, and past fixed points like a staircase or a fireplace hearth.

Engineered hardwood for a main-floor renovation in Brantford typically runs $9–$16 per sq ft installed, and luxury vinyl plank runs $6–$11 per sq ft installed, including subfloor prep. Subfloor levelling — needed more often than homeowners expect once a wall comes out and two rooms with slightly different floor heights become one continuous space — can add $2,000–$6,000 depending on how much of the main level is affected.

If you're staging a main-floor renovation over more than one phase, order enough flooring for the entire connected space up front, even if you're only installing part of it now. Discontinued dye lots are one of the most common reasons a "matching" floor two years later doesn't actually match.

Structural changes and engineering

Most main-floor renovations in Brantford involve removing at least one wall, and whether that wall is load-bearing changes the project significantly. A non-load-bearing partition wall can often come down for a few thousand dollars in demo and drywall patching. A load-bearing wall requires a structural engineer to size a replacement beam, a permit, and in some cases temporary shoring while the beam goes in — typically adding $8,000–$20,000 to the project depending on the span and whether the beam needs to be exposed or hidden in the ceiling.

We handle the structural engineering and permit submission as part of the project scope, but the timeline needs to account for it — engineering review and permit approval typically add two to five weeks before framing work can start on a wall removal.

What changes in older Brantford and Paris homes

Brantford and Paris both have a meaningful stock of homes built before 1980, and that changes the math on a main-floor renovation more than on a contained kitchen project — because opening a wall or lifting a floor in an older home tends to expose more than the drawings show. Plaster-and-lath walls behind newer drywall, knob-and-tube wiring still live in a wall you're about to open, and uneven or settled subfloor framing all turn up regularly once demo starts.

We generally recommend budgeting a 10–15% contingency on a main-floor renovation in a pre-1980 Brantford or Paris home, versus 5–10% for a newer build. It isn't a prediction that something will go wrong — it's what lets a real surprise get absorbed without stalling the schedule or the budget.

What to do before you request an estimate

  • Decide whether you want the kitchen open to the living room, or whether staying within the existing footprint would work just as well.
  • Check whether the flooring in adjoining rooms would need to be replaced to match a new kitchen floor.
  • Note any walls, closets or the powder room location that might need to shift as part of an open layout.
  • Set aside a written contingency line, especially if the home was built before 1980.
  • Book a site visit instead of asking for a number over the phone — a wall's structural role can't be confirmed without seeing it.
